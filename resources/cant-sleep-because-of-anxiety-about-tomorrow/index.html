Can't Sleep Because of Anxiety About Tomorrow? Try This

Lying in bed replaying tomorrow's meeting, deadline or difficult conversation is exhausting, and the harder you try to sleep, the more awake you can feel. The steps below are practical habits many people find helpful. They are not a treatment, but they can make nights feel more manageable.

Get tomorrow out of your head before bed

A lot of night-time worry is your brain trying not to forget something. Give it a safe place to put those thoughts. About an hour before bed, spend a few minutes writing down everything on your mind about tomorrow, then next to each item jot the first small step you will take, or simply 'deal with this tomorrow.'

The point is not to solve everything. It is to tell yourself that the worry has been captured and has a time slot, so you do not have to hold it all night.

A worry list you can copy

Tomorrow I am worried about:
1. [worry]  ->  First step: [small action]  ->  When: [time]
2. [worry]  ->  First step: [small action]  ->  When: [time]
3. [worry]  ->  First step: [small action]  ->  When: [time]

Things I cannot control tonight: [list them]
One thing that went okay today: [one line]

Wind down your body, not just your mind

  • Keep a regular bedtime and wake time where you can, including most weekends.
  • Dim the lights and step away from work email and stressful news in the last hour.
  • Try slow breathing, making each out-breath a little longer than the in-breath.
  • Tense and release muscle groups one at a time, from your feet up to your face.
  • Keep the bedroom cool, dark and quiet, or use a fan or soft background sound.
  • Notice how caffeine late in the day and alcohol in the evening affect your own sleep, and adjust if they seem to make things worse.

If you are still awake

If you have been lying awake for what feels like a long while, it often helps to get up rather than wrestle with it. Go to another room, keep the lights low and do something quiet and dull, like reading a few pages of a familiar book or folding laundry. Go back to bed when you feel sleepy.

Try not to check the clock. Calculating how many hours are left tends to add pressure. Turn the clock away from you. It can also help to remind yourself that one rough night is unpleasant but survivable, and that you have gotten through busy days on little sleep before.

Make tomorrow feel smaller

Anticipation is often heavier than the event itself. Earlier in the evening, prepare what you can: lay out clothes, pack your bag, review your notes once, and set your alarm. Then decide that preparation is finished for the night.

If the same kind of worry comes back night after night, try picking a short worry time earlier in the day. When the thought shows up at night, remind yourself you have a time for it tomorrow afternoon.

When a worry keeps looping, it can help to ask yourself a few gentle questions: What is the most likely way tomorrow goes? What would I do if the hard part happened? Who could I ask for help? You are not trying to argue the worry away, only to remind yourself that you have options and have handled hard days before.

When to get help

If trouble sleeping goes on for weeks, if worry is affecting your daytime life, or if you notice changes in your mood, appetite or energy, talk to your doctor or a licensed mental health professional. Ongoing sleep problems and anxiety are common, and there are effective approaches a professional can help you with. Do not start or stop any medication or supplement for sleep without advice from a doctor or pharmacist.

If you are in crisis, in the US call or text 988.
