How to Care for an Aging Parent While Working Full Time

Caring for a parent while holding down a full-time job can feel like working two jobs, with the second one full of hard emotions. You cannot do everything alone, and you should not have to. These steps can help you get organized, share the load and look after yourself along the way.

Get a clear picture of what is needed

Start by listing what your parent actually needs help with, now and in the near future. Think about medical appointments, medications, meals, housework, transport, bills and paperwork, personal care, and company. Ask your parent what matters most to them, and involve them in decisions as much as possible.

A clear list turns a vague, overwhelming responsibility into tasks that can be shared, scheduled or handed off. It also makes conversations with siblings, employers and professionals much easier.

A care planning checklist

  • Keep a shared document with doctors' names, medication list, allergies, insurance details and emergency contacts.
  • Make a shared calendar for appointments and visits that family members can see and add to.
  • Know where important documents are kept, such as IDs, insurance cards and any legal or financial papers.
  • Ask your parent's doctor or care team which tasks need hands-on help and which could be done by phone or online.
  • Find out what local support exists through your area's agency on aging or local council, such as meal programs, transport, adult day programs or home care.
  • Talk with your parent, while they can take part, about their wishes for future care, and consider speaking with an elder law attorney about documents like powers of attorney.

Talk to your employer

Many people try to hide caregiving at work until it becomes a crisis. If you feel safe doing so, it often helps to talk with your manager or HR early. Ask what options exist, such as flexible hours, remote work, adjusted schedules, leave or an employee assistance program. Policies vary a lot, so ask specifically rather than assuming.

Go into the conversation with a proposal, not just a problem. Show how your work will still get done.

A message to your manager

Hi [manager's name], I wanted to give you a heads-up that I am now helping care for my [mother/father], which includes some appointments during work hours. I am committed to keeping my work on track, and I would like to talk about options like [shifting my hours on certain days / working remotely on appointment days]. Could we find a time this week to discuss? I am also happy to talk with HR about any policies that apply.

Share the load

If you have siblings or other relatives, hold a family conversation, in person or by video, to divide responsibilities. People can contribute in different ways: some with hands-on time, some with money, some with research, phone calls or paperwork. Write down who is doing what so it does not all quietly fall back on you.

Accept specific offers of help from friends and neighbors, and have a short list ready of things people can do, like a meal drop-off, a ride to an appointment or a visit so you can have an afternoon off. Professional services such as home care aides or respite care can also give you regular breaks.

When to get help

Caregivers often put their own health last. Keep your own medical appointments, protect some sleep, and make time for at least one thing that is just for you. Caregiver support groups, in person or online, can be a place to be understood without explaining.

If you are feeling exhausted, hopeless, resentful or unable to cope, talk to your doctor or a licensed counselor. Those feelings are common among caregivers and do not make you a bad son or daughter. For questions about your parent's care, rely on their doctors and care team, and for legal or financial planning, consult a qualified professional.

If you are in crisis, in the US call or text 988.
