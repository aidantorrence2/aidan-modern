How to Make Friends After Moving to a New City

Moving somewhere new strips away the easy friendships that school, old jobs and long-standing neighbors used to provide. As an adult you have to build those ties on purpose, which can feel awkward at first. The good news is that friendship mostly comes from showing up repeatedly, and that part you can plan.

Choose places you will return to

One-off events are fine, but friendships tend to form through repeated contact with the same people. A weekly class, a running club, a volunteer shift or a regular pub quiz gives you the chance to see familiar faces again and again until chatting feels natural.

Pick one or two things you would enjoy even if you met no one, and commit to them for a couple of months. That way the time is never wasted, and you are not carrying the pressure of making every visit count.

Places worth trying

  • Recurring classes: pottery, climbing, language exchange, improv, cooking.
  • Sports and activity groups: running clubs, rec leagues, hiking groups, cycling rides.
  • Volunteering on a regular schedule, such as a food bank, animal shelter or community garden.
  • Hobby meetups: board game nights, book clubs, knitting circles, photography walks.
  • Your workplace or coworking space, especially optional lunches and after-work events.
  • Faith communities or cultural associations, if they fit your life.
  • Friend-finding apps and local online groups for your neighborhood or interests.

Be the one who follows up

Most people in a new city are waiting for someone else to make the first move. Be that person. If you have a good conversation, ask to swap numbers or connect online, then send a message within a few days while it is still fresh.

Suggest something specific and easy rather than a vague 'we should hang out sometime.' Specific plans are much easier to say yes to.

It can feel forward, but most people are flattered to be asked. If someone says they are busy, try once more another time with a different plan. If they never suggest an alternative, that is useful information too, and it frees you to put your energy into people who respond warmly.

A message you can send

Hey [name], it was great chatting at [place] about [topic]. I am still pretty new here and trying to explore more of the city. Want to grab a coffee or check out [specific place or event] sometime next week?

Turn acquaintances into friends

  • Invite people more than once. A no is often about timing, not about you.
  • Host something low effort, like a potluck, a game night or a picnic in the park.
  • Ask people to bring a friend. Your circle grows faster when it overlaps with theirs.
  • Remember small details and ask about them next time: the job interview, the visiting parents, the race they were training for.
  • Share a little about yourself too. Closeness grows when both people open up gradually.
  • Keep in touch with old friends while you build new ones. Calls home can steady you through the slow stretch.

Be patient with the lonely middle

There is often a stretch where you know lots of people by name but no one well enough to call on a bad day. That is a normal stage, not a sign that it is not working. Close friendships tend to take a lot of time together to develop, so keep showing up even when it feels slow.

Be kind to yourself on the quiet weekends. Explore the city alone, find a favorite cafe, and treat it as getting to know your new home. If loneliness starts to feel heavy for weeks at a time, or it is affecting your sleep, work or mood, talking with a counselor or your doctor is a reasonable step, and you do not need to wait until things are bad.
