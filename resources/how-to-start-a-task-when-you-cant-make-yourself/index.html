How to Start a Task When You Can't Make Yourself

Staring at a task you know you need to do, while somehow doing anything else, is frustrating and common. It is rarely about laziness. Usually the task feels too big, too vague or too unpleasant to begin. The trick is to make starting so small and clear that it barely feels like a decision.

Figure out what is blocking you

Different kinds of stuck need different fixes. Ask yourself which of these fits. Is the task vague, so you do not know where to begin? Is it big, so it feels endless? Is it boring or unpleasant? Are you worried about doing it badly? Or are you simply tired?

Naming the block takes some of the fog out of it, and points to the right tool. Vague tasks need a clear first step. Big tasks need breaking down. Unpleasant ones need a time limit or a reward. Fear of doing it badly needs permission to do a rough version.

Shrink the first step

Instead of 'write the report,' make the first step 'open the document and type the title.' Instead of 'clean the kitchen,' make it 'put the dishes in the sink.' A good first step is physical, specific and takes only a couple of minutes.

Once you have started, keeping going is often much easier than it looked from the outside. And if you stop after the first step, you have still done more than before.

For bigger projects, write out the next few steps in the same way, each one small and concrete. Keep the list somewhere you will see it. When you sit down again tomorrow, you will not have to work out where to begin, which is often the hardest part of all.

A task-starting template

The task: [what needs doing]
Why it matters: [one sentence]
What is blocking me: [vague / big / boring / scary / tired]

The smallest first step: [physical, specific, a couple of minutes]
When I will do it: [time, or right after a specific habit]
I will stop after: [a short time limit]
Afterwards I will: [small reward or break]

Use a short timer

Tell yourself you only have to work for a short, set amount of time, then you are allowed to stop. Set a timer and begin. Knowing there is an end makes it easier to start, and many people find that once the timer goes off they want to keep going.

If you do stop, that is fine. Take a proper break, then decide whether to do another round. Several short rounds add up.

Change your surroundings

  • Put your phone in another room or turn on a focus mode.
  • Close every tab and app you do not need for the task.
  • Lay out what you need in advance, like the document already open or the cleaning supplies on the counter.
  • Work alongside someone, in person or on a video call, even if they are doing their own thing.
  • Try a different spot, such as a cafe, a library or another room.
  • Pair the task with something pleasant, like music or a favorite drink.
  • Tell someone what you plan to do and when, and let them know when you have done it.

Be kind to yourself, and know when to get help

Criticizing yourself for procrastinating usually makes the task feel heavier, not lighter. Treat yourself the way you would a friend who is stuck: acknowledge it is hard, then help them take one small step.

If you are stuck most of the time, across many areas of life, or if difficulty starting comes with low mood, constant worry or exhaustion, it is worth talking to your doctor or a licensed mental health professional. They can help you understand what is going on and find support that fits.

If you are in crisis, in the US call or text 988.
