Do I need a permit to shoot here? A checklist for outdoor sessions

Permit rules differ park by park, not city by city, and the fine or the eviction usually lands mid-session. This is the checklist to run before you confirm a location with a client. It is not legal advice; it is where to look and what to ask.

Find the owner first

  • City parks: the city's parks and recreation department. Search the park name plus 'photography permit' and the department's own site, not a forum.
  • County and state parks: the park's own page usually has a 'commercial photography' or 'special use' section with a fee and a lead time.
  • Botanic gardens, arboretums, historic houses: almost always a permit, often with a per-session fee and limited slots. Book early in autumn.
  • Beaches and waterfronts: may be city, county, state or a port authority. The lifeguard tower sign often names the agency.
  • Streets and plazas: usually fine for a small crew without equipment on the ground; tripods, lights and blocking the pavement change the answer. Private plazas that look public are private.

Ask the office these questions

  • Does a single photographer with one client and no lights or tripod need a permit, or only crews?
  • What is the fee, and is it per session, per day or annual?
  • How many days' notice, and is there a form or an email?
  • Are there blackout dates, closed areas or hours when permits are not issued?
  • Does the permit need insurance, and what minimum?
  • Can the permit be transferred to a backup date if it rains?

Tell the client

Put the permit status and fee in the booking notes and the invoice. If the location needs one, say who pays and that the date depends on approval. If it does not need one, say that too, so there is no surprise if a ranger asks.

Keep your own list

Every time you check a location, record the rule, fee, lead time, link and the date you checked. In two seasons you will have the only accurate permit list for your city, and you will stop re-checking the same park every spring.
